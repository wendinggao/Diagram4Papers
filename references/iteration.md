# Review the rendered image, repair the design decision

Keep a short content plan and an actual visual review log in the figure workspace. Save revisions when useful. A new dense composition generally needs several native-render/review rounds; a narrow edit needs proportionate verification. Do not manufacture findings or edit counts.

## Review in order

1. **Method truth.** Read the figure as a reader. Verify objects, transformations, branches, bindings and example outputs against sources. Does a concrete change explain the main mechanism?
2. **Composition.** Inspect the full canvas before zooming. Find the dominant scene and reading path. Uniform cards, long text corridors, giant labels or unchanged repeated objects may be structural problems, not spacing problems.
3. **Useful density.** Inspect each region for meaningful payload, illustration size and avoidable idle area. Increase useful information or reflow the region; do not manufacture method detail or use decorative complexity as filler.
4. **Assets and colors.** Compare visible icon mass and detail with the target. Check local color combinations and colored area. Source/license compliance alone does not establish visual adequacy.
5. **Local hygiene.** Inspect text, symbols, scientific and workflow edges, frames, alignment and margins in actual native-rendered details. Check at realistic viewing size.
6. **Regression.** Re-render after changes. Confirm previously accepted content and style remain intact, and XML/PNG/ledger represent the same revision.

Record location, observed problem, cause, patch and outcome. Distinguish native-rendered evidence from static-check results. Don't claim visual review from reading XML or numerical scores. The default public exemplar is a comparison anchor, not a claim of universal best layout.

## Turn visual feedback into a drawing decision

Read feedback in the context of the visible region. Choose the repair that changes its underlying composition, then compare the same crop and the full canvas at a common display scale.

| Feedback / visible evidence | Productive edit | What to look for after rendering |
|---|---|---|
| "Too many text boxes" | Convert the key relation into a mapping, candidate comparison, dependency graph or worked transition; keep its short labels | The operation can be followed through objects and a visible consequence |
| "Repetitive" / repeated input scenes | Keep an establishing scene and replace later copies with the relevant state fields or a focused detail | Each occurrence advances the explanation; the figure gains variety without losing identity |
| "Empty" / content floats in a broad panel | Add an omitted supported intermediate when useful, or reflow/narrow the panel; enlarge its meaningful scene and icons together | More of the area explains something; text remains readable and the canvas becomes tighter |
| "Messy" / individually legible items have weak ownership | Group by operation, align rows and section edges, and strengthen the appropriate internal dashed tier | The groups are recognizable at normal viewing size and the reading order is clear |
| "Flat" / everything looks like an equal card | Make the main mechanism dominant and compress supporting entry/output actions; mix open scenes with compact structured rows | A clear focal region and varied but orderly rhythm |
| "Lightweight" / tiny icons and faint content | Enlarge alpha-visible icon subjects and scientific objects, coordinate dark contours and text weight, tighten local composition | Contents gain substance while large frames remain subordinate |
| "More icons" / headers are illustrated but operations are plain | Select professional assets for the actual interior actions and objects; place them beside their payloads and resize the local group | Illustrations participate throughout the explanation at a useful visible size |
| "Colors clash" / large auxiliary blocks compete | Reduce their area/saturation, lighten fills and coordinate adjacent outlines while retaining small multicolor details | The module identity remains clear and internal colors feel naturally related |
| "Too monochrome" | Restore original icon colors and introduce controlled auxiliary hues on local payloads | Richer contents over a quiet ground, with legible object/state distinctions |
| "Too many lines" / long routes dominate | Use adjacency or reading order for obvious succession; shorten necessary local edges and clarify branch destinations; reclaim routing space | The reader can trace the important relationship without following an ornamental tour |
| Peer results or sections feel inconsistent | Match their semantic frame tier, text style, baseline and centering, even if their physical sizes differ | Peers read as peers and hierarchy comes from meaning |

Fullness and order are complementary: aligned subgroups let substantial objects sit close together. Color variety and harmony are also complementary: pale large surfaces support detailed multicolor objects. Evaluate both qualities after each repair rather than maximizing one in isolation.

For example, a plain "retrieve" card can become three compact candidate rows with a criterion, one selected row and its exposed program. A crowded verification area can become a clearly bounded grid whose cells pair an operation icon with specific evidence. Neither improvement comes from simply increasing the number of triangles, cards, icons or words.

## Scope of corrections

For style-only changes freeze wording and scientific relationships. For layout-only changes freeze symbols and example values, then recheck affected connectors. For a replaced example follow all dependent formulas, parameters, calls, checks and outputs. Do not equate scalar variables with point roles during cosmetic normalization.

After a successful change, carry its design relation forward: preserve the clarified grouping while refining its colors, or preserve a readable example while simplifying labels. Apply shared typography and frame tokens by semantic role across related figures; their layouts and palettes can remain different. A one-off request to change a particular shape, label or color does not become a universal design rule.

## Skill validation versus package validation

A passing parser/test suite validates tooling, not the skill's design behavior. Test one public mechanism-rich case end to end: read sources, plan content, select real assets, build and render, critique against the style target, revise. Then test a different method using its own layout. A documented public exemplar is a design anchor and smoke test; it does not prove transfer or autonomous agent performance.

When delegation is unavailable, explicitly label this as author-run validation. Do not fabricate an independent evaluator. Keep remaining quality limitations concrete. No perfect scores, minimum defect quotas or guaranteed user acceptance.
